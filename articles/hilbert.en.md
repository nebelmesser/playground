# The Shape of Time in the Hilbert Clock

Analog clocks show time through the position of their hands; digital clocks show it through changing numbers. Both make it easy to tell the time, but neither conveys how time feels: how it builds up, how each moment differs from the last. The hands make another circle, the numbers change. A stretch of time leaves no image of its own on either kind of clock face.

In the [Hilbert clock](/hilbert/clock.html), each moment has its own unique shape. Time gradually fills a map: past hours form colored regions, the future stays dark, and the present is marked in red. As time moves on, the edge of the filled area changes. The present gets a pattern of its own, something you can look at.

An ordinary progress bar also shows how much time has passed. But it fills in a straight line: the bar just gets longer. It is boring to watch because every step looks like the last. All you can do is wait for it to reach the end. In the Hilbert clock, the path keeps turning, forming bulges, filling gaps, and changing the outline of the whole. Each step brings a new shape worth following. Your attention shifts from waiting for the end to seeing what time looks like now.

The clock uses a variation of the Hilbert curve. It lays out a sequence of moments on a plane, turning again and again through smaller and smaller parts of a grid. Each cell stands for a short stretch of time. Within the chosen map, every step adds a cell to the filled area, so its full shape never repeats. The detail of this pattern depends on the scale: zoom in and shorter intervals become visible.

What interests me here is the chance to see how even an ordinary moment is unique. Nothing remarkable may be happening while you look at the screen. But this moment already has an outline that sets it apart from the one before and the one after. You can pause to look at it, notice a bulge or a gap, wait for a small area to fill. Time that would have been easy to overlook takes on a visible shape.

This shape is made from all the time that has passed since the start of the chosen period. The present sits at the edge of what has built up. You can see an image of your own history in this: every new moment arrives with everything that came before it. The clock knows nothing about our lives, but its design gives this thought a visible form. Duration becomes material for an image.

Scale adds another dimension to this. You can look at a day, a month, a year, or a period of your own choosing. Beside the full map, a magnified view shows the area around the present. On the map of a year, movement is almost invisible, while inside one tiny part of it, new shapes keep appearing. One moment has several different forms in space. Its details become visible when we give them enough room.

For me, this is an example of the [computational sublime](sublime.en.md). We know the rule behind the pattern and can explain every step, while watching it gives us a way to think about uniqueness, memory, and our own attention. The sense of the sublime comes from the many shapes that emerge, one after another, from a single simple rule. Each has its own place in time; we only manage to notice a few.

You can leave the clock open and choose one of these shapes, pause with it, and follow how it changes. Perhaps later it will remind you of what was happening during those minutes. A mathematically defined pattern can then take on a personal meaning: this is what that moment looked like, and I saw it.
