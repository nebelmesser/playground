# Computational Sublime

Philosophy too often resembles the study of sedimentary rock. Commentary piles up on commentary, and concepts carry traces of old disputes. To reach the question itself, you first have to work out what one author meant when arguing with another several centuries ago. The history of thought gradually takes the place of thinking.

Esotericism is stuck in the past in its own way. It still maps human experience through the classical elements, planets, and alchemical transformations. These images once grew out of what people knew about the world. That knowledge has changed, but the symbols remain much the same, with words like “quantum” and “energy” thrown in. The images of ancient science survived, but the habit of creating new ones was lost.

Today's knowledge offers material for both philosophy and poetic thinking. Mathematics and computing let us build systems we can interact with: changing conditions, observing consequences, finding connections, and interpreting results. Questions about cause and effect, freedom, order, and meaning become part of this experience. As we explore a system, we also explore our own ways of seeing and making sense of things.

There is a parallel here with contemplative practices. Someone watching a body decompose already knows they will die. The practice makes that knowledge felt in a different way. What interests us is the method itself: how sustained attention to something changes our relationship to it and to our own experience.

Magical practices can be approached in much the same way. A ritual arranges space, attention, actions, symbols, and expectations. We can study its effects on a participant without accepting the tradition's beliefs about the universe. The question then is what happens to the person inside the pentagram.

The computational sublime brings together observation, action, and interpretation. A person asks a question, sets conditions, gets a result, and tries again. Along the way, they notice their own part in the process: what they expect, what draws their attention, which connections they make, and what they find meaningful.

This practice has an analytical side and a poetic one. The analytical side explains the rules of the system, how a result was produced, and which conclusions it supports. The poetic side connects what happens with human experience. The way a system is built, how it behaves, or an image it produces can help someone recognize and describe something in their own life.

Modern knowledge lets us build new metaphors from the relationships we study: between chance and order, repetition and change, a single element and the larger structure. These relationships give us a language for talking about habits, choices, identity, and society. The better we understand them, the more our metaphors can express. A scientific concept used as an impressive label adds little.

Meaning can also emerge through the act of interpretation. When we encounter a chance combination of images or words, we look for connections, draw on memories, and choose an interpretation. Those choices reveal our concerns and expectations. The system does not need to know anything about us: our own response gives us a way to understand ourselves.

Poetic thinking has its own kind of precision. Does an image help us tell experiences apart, notice a connection, or put words to something that was vague? Wanting to influence events and wanting to know all their consequences in advance can both be called a need for control. A good image helps us see the difference. We can develop it, refine it, or discard it if it no longer fits our experience.

Explanations plant seeds for reflection. They show how a system works, suggest questions and comparisons, and help us notice what we might otherwise miss. But if we announce in advance exactly what someone is supposed to understand, exploration soon becomes a search for evidence to support a ready-made claim. People need room to find their own reading, including one the creator did not expect.

No matter how compelling a metaphor is, it does not become a law of nature. Recognizing yourself in an image is not the same as receiving proof, a diagnosis, or a prediction. Keeping this distinction lets us work freely with symbols and rituals without crediting a system with knowledge it does not have.

When designing these systems, we need to consider both their rules and how people use them: what a person can do, how long they spend with a result, and where their attention goes. These choices shape a practice in which today's knowledge becomes material for personal experience, and personal experience leads to new questions.
